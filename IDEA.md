# Will the lander survive?

**Challenge:** Lunar Landing Site Navigator (NASA Space Apps 2026, Port Louis)
**Status:** idea proposal, nothing built yet. Team to decide.

## The problem

The Moon's south pole has ice, so space agencies want to land there. It is a hard place to land:

- The Sun never rises high. It skims the edge of the sky, so a hill or crater rim can block it for days.
- A lander runs on solar panels. No sunlight means the battery drains and the lander dies.
- To talk to the lander, it must be able to see Earth in its sky. If a hill hides Earth, there is no contact.

Where and when you land decides whether the mission lives. The tools experts use to check this today are complicated and hard to read.

## Our idea

A simple website. You pick a landing spot and a landing date, and it shows:

1. **The view from the lander.** The hills around it, with the Sun and Earth moving across the sky and going behind them.
2. **The verdict.** A battery that fills in sunlight and drains in the dark, ending in "survived" or "died on day X".
3. **The safe dates.** A green and red calendar showing what happens if the launch is a few days late.
4. **The Mauritius moment.** When the lander can see Earth while the Moon is in the sky over Port Louis.

It also works on a phone and can be added to the home screen. A separate mobile app is not required by the rules and is not planned.

## Why this can win

| Judging criterion | Our answer |
|---|---|
| Impact | Turns hours of sunlight into a clear result: the mission lives or dies |
| Creativity | Battery survival run, launch-delay calendar, Mauritius link |
| Validity | Built on NASA data; our sunlight numbers checked against NASA's published sunlight maps |
| Relevance | Compares sites and dates, shows Sun and Earth against the horizon, covers power and contact with Earth |
| Presentation | One screen, one question, understood in five seconds |

Most other teams worldwide show charts of visibility hours. We show an answer.

## NASA data we plan to use

- NASA's height map of the Moon's south pole (from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's laser altimeter, LOLA). This tells us where the hills are.
- NASA/JPL Horizons. This tells us where the Sun and Earth are in the Moon's sky on any date.
- NASA's published sunlight maps of the south pole, to check our results.

To confirm: these must match the Resources tab on the official challenge page.

## What we must be honest about

- The battery is a simple model, not a real lander design.
- "Earth is visible" does not guarantee a working radio link.
- The check against NASA's maps is planned, not done yet.
- This is a fast first look for planners, teachers and the public. It does not replace NASA's professional tools.
